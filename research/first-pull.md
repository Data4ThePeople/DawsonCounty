# First pull: Lexington, Dawson County and the Tyson plant

Gathered October 13, 2026, in a session run inside the AffordableHousing
project before this project existed. Carried over here unchanged, apart from
the last section. Census and BLS figures were pulled directly
(`scripts/01_first_pull.py`, raw output in `data/raw/`) and reproduced from
this project the same day. Everything else is from the linked reporting, read
as a summary and not yet checked against the page. Nothing here has been
through a tie-out.

Eric's outline for the post: the history of the town; the metrics on the town
(population and so on); when Tyson came and what it meant to the town; why
they closed and the rationale they gave.

## 1. History of the town

- **Plum Creek.** Lexington began as Plum Creek, a stage and Pony Express stop
  on the south side of the Platte. A trading post went up in 1860 and Fort
  Plum Creek in 1864.
- **The railroad.** The Union Pacific arrived in 1866 and the settlement moved
  north of the river to meet it. On August 7, 1867, Cheyenne warriors derailed
  a freight train about 3.5 miles west of town, and three railroad employees
  were killed.
- **County and name.** Dawson County was created January 11, 1860, and
  organized July 11, 1871. Plum Creek became the county seat in 1873, and the
  town voted to rename itself Lexington in 1889.
- **The combine plant.** Sperry New Holland came in 1975 and employed about
  900 people at its early-1980s peak. It announced a temporary closure on
  March 14, 1985, and shut for good on October 24, 1985, ending 940 jobs and a
  payroll of more than $16 million.

## 3. When Tyson came, and what it meant

- **Arrival.** Gov. Kay Orr announced on November 27, 1988, that IBP would buy
  the empty combine plant. It was an early recipient of state tax incentives
  under LB 775. Production began November 8, 1990, with 1,200 to 1,300
  workers. Tyson acquired IBP in 2001.
- **Rough early years.** Worker turnover averaged 250%, and by 1992 IBP had
  hired and fired a workforce equal to three-quarters of the town's
  population. School enrollment rose 50%. Latino workers made up 70% to 80% of
  the plant's workforce in a 1997 study (Gouveia and Stull).
- **Later arrivals.** Lexington had 10 African-born residents in 2000 and 829
  by 2016. In 2018 the high school's 880 students spoke 30 languages.
- **Share of the county's jobs.** The plant's 3,212 positions (WARN count)
  equal about 27% of all jobs located in Dawson County in March 2025 (11,868)
  and 34% of private jobs (9,444). This share is our calculation from BLS QCEW
  and the WARN count, not a published figure.
- **Schools.** Nearly half of Lexington students had a parent at the plant,
  according to school officials (AP).
- **Taxes.** The city manager, Joe Pepplitsch, says Tyson paid no city taxes
  under a deal negotiated years ago. The terms were not found.

## 4. Why they closed, and the rationale given

- **The announcement.** On November 21, 2025, Tyson said it would close
  Lexington effective January 20, 2026, and cut Amarillo to one shift. Its
  stated reason was to "right size its beef business and position it for
  long-term success."
- **Cattle supply.** Tyson tied the move to a historically low U.S. cattle
  herd. USDA counted 86.2 million head on January 1, 2026.
- **Losses.** The beef segment's adjusted operating loss was $291 million in
  fiscal 2024 and $426 million in fiscal 2025. Including legal accruals and a
  goodwill write-off, the fiscal 2025 loss was $1.135 billion. In November
  2025 Tyson forecast a fiscal 2026 beef loss of $400 million to $600 million.
- **The CEO.** On February 2, 2026, Donnie King said: "We did not make this
  [decision] lightly," and "Continuing to absorb losses like we have been
  seeing for the past few years is simply unacceptable."
- **Why Lexington specifically.** No reason was found from Tyson itself. Gov.
  Pillen said he was told the key factor was the age and inefficiency of a
  plant built for farm machinery, and that Tyson asked for no incentives to
  stay. The plant was running about 3,600 to 3,700 head a day against a
  capacity of 5,000 (about 4.8% of daily U.S. beef slaughter, per UNL).
- **It was not the last.** In August 2026 Tyson said it would close plants in
  Joslin, Illinois, and Eagle Mountain, Utah, sell Pasco, Washington, and
  restore Amarillo's second shift.

## What has happened since

- **Unemployment.** The county rate went from 3.4% in January 2026 to 16.9% in
  February and peaked at 20.1% in March. August is 16.4% (preliminary). BLS
  LAUS, not seasonally adjusted; October 2025 is blank in the series.
- **Workers.** Employed residents fell from 13,292 in August 2025 to 9,941 in
  August 2026, down 25%. The labor force fell 13%.
- **Jobs in the county.** Manufacturing jobs fell from 3,997 in March 2025 to
  677 in March 2026, down 83%. Private jobs fell 40% (9,444 to 5,673). BLS
  QCEW.
- **Schools.** Enrollment is down 9.3% this fall, from 3,172 to 2,877, at the
  low end of the 150 to 750 the district feared.
- **The site.** In April 2026 the city agreed to buy Tyson's wastewater plant,
  land and rail access. No buyer for the plant itself has been reported.
- **Projections made at the time.** UNL (Dennis and Thompson, December 22,
  2025): 7,003 jobs lost statewide including the 3,212 at the plant, $530
  million a year in labor income, $3.3 billion in output. Kansas City Fed:
  county unemployment "might rise from 3% in October 2025 to 27% within three
  years."

## Not verified yet

- **Blocked sources.** The Nebraska State Historical Society article and the
  Nebraska Examiner's announcement-day story returned errors. The 1997
  Gouveia and Stull study PDF downloaded but could not be read (no PDF text
  tool installed); only its abstract was read.
- **Conflicts in the reporting.** The founding year is given as 1871 or 1873.
  The Sperry closure is 1985 in Nebraska sources and 1986 in one national
  report. Loss figures vary by outlet depending on which measure they use.
- **Pay.** The plant's pay is unclear: UNL assumes $94,169 per worker with
  benefits, AP's total ($241 million) works out to about $75,000, and BLS
  shows county manufacturing wages near $50,000 a year.
- **Missing pieces.** Tyson's full original press release text; the 1990
  Hispanic share; the terms of the city tax deal.
- **Data traps already seen.** QCEW withholds the food manufacturing and
  meatpacking rows for the county in every quarter pulled except food
  manufacturing in 2026 Q1, so the plant's own job count cannot be read from
  BLS. ACS 2020-2024 for a city of about 10,900 carries wide margins of error,
  which were not pulled.
